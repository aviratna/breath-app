The idea behind Breath

Every being is born with a fixed number of breaths.

So goes an old teaching, told in many forms across India and the Himalaya — by yogis counting the breath, by monks keeping death in mind. A life is not measured in years, but in breaths. Each one spent is one fewer.

We think we have time

Few lines capture the feeling as well as one that is often attributed to the Buddha:

“The trouble is, you think you have time.”

Popularly attributed to the Buddha; from Jack Kornfield, Buddha’s Little Instruction Book (1994)

Pare ca na vijānanti, mayam ettha yamāmase;
ye ca tattha vijānanti, tato sammanti medhagā.

“Others do not realise that we are all heading towards death. Those who do realise it — their quarrels come to rest.”

Dhammapada, verse 6 (Pāli)

The pause we stopped taking

Today we move faster than ever. Our days are full, our phones fuller. We answer everything except the quiet thought of someone we love — and tell ourselves there will be time later.

Somewhere in the hurry we forget what we are running for. Breath is a small way to stop: to notice that the day is passing, breath by breath, and to spend one of those breaths on the person who just crossed your mind.

A drop at the tip of a leaf

We plan our lives in decades. The Yoga Vāsiṣṭha, in which the young prince Rāma questions the meaning of life, sees it differently:

आयुः पल्लवकोणाग्रलम्बाम्बुकणभङ्गुरम्

āyuḥ pallava-koṇāgra-lambāmbu-kaṇa-bhaṅguram

“Life is as fragile as a drop of water hanging at the tip of a leaf.”

Yoga Vāsiṣṭha, Vairāgya Prakaraṇa 1.14.1 (Sanskrit)

Breath is life

In the yoga traditions, breath — prāṇa — is life itself. The Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā says it plainly:

यावद्वायुः स्थितो देहे तावज्जीवनमुच्यते ।
मरणं तस्य निष्क्रान्तिः ततो वायुं निरोधयेत् ॥

yāvad vāyuḥ sthito dehe tāvaj jīvanam ucyate,
maraṇaṃ tasya niṣkrāntiḥ tato vāyuṃ nirodhayet.

“As long as breath remains in the body, that is called life. Death is its leaving. Therefore, one should learn to restrain the breath.”

Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā 2.3 (Sanskrit)

Between one breath and the next

In the Maraṇassati Sutta, the Buddha asks his monks how they keep death in mind. One hopes to live another day; another, the length of a meal. He praises the monk who wishes only this:

Aho vatāhaṃ tadantaraṃ jīveyyaṃ yadantaraṃ assasitvā vā passasāmi, passasitvā vā assasāmi.

“If only I might live for the time it takes to breathe in after breathing out, or to breathe out after breathing in.”

Aṅguttara Nikāya 6.19, Maraṇassati Sutta (Pāli)

Mindfulness of breathing — ānāpānasati (Majjhima Nikāya 118) — rests on the same ground: each breath is a doorway back to the present moment.

Which comes first?

Tibetan teachers keep impermanence — མི་རྟག་པ་, mi rtag pa — close with a saying repeated in monasteries and homes alike:

སང་དང་ཕྱི་མ་གང་སྔ་ཆ་མེད།

sang dang phyi ma gang snga cha med

“Tomorrow or the next life — which comes first, we never know.”

Tibetan saying

Why give one away

Counting breaths is not meant to frighten. Breath turns that attention outward. When someone crosses your mind, you spend one of today’s breaths on them. It is small, it is finite, and it costs you something — which is what makes it sincere.

There is no message to write and no reply expected. In both the yogic and Buddhist traditions, giving without expecting anything back — dāna, दान — is where generosity begins.

What the count is, and isn’t

Your count is a contemplative estimate: about fifteen breaths a minute, from now until midnight, less the breaths you have given today. It is not a measurement of your breathing and not a prediction of anything. It is a device for attention.
